What Longevity Supplements Actually Have Human Evidence Behind Them (2026)

RG
By Rashtriya Gaurav

If you spend any time in longevity circles, you've seen the stacks: NMN, resveratrol, fisetin, spermidine, berberine, a dozen others, sometimes alongside prescription drugs people are sourcing themselves. The problem is that "extends lifespan in mice" and "extends lifespan in you" are very different claims, and most headlines blur the two.

Here's a level-headed look at where the evidence actually stands right now — not as a sales pitch, but as a filter you can run any supplement through before you buy it.

The core problem: mouse data isn't human data

Most "this compound extends lifespan" headlines trace back to studies in mice, worms, or isolated cells. Those models are useful for finding mechanisms, but they've historically translated poorly to humans. The gold-standard mouse testing program run by the National Institute on Aging has flagged a handful of compounds — rapamycin, acarbose, 17-alpha-estradiol — that reliably extend mouse lifespan. Notably, none of those are over-the-counter supplements; they're prescription drugs, and self-sourcing them carries real risk (rapamycin in particular is an immunosuppressant).

So the first filter for any longevity supplement claim: is this backed by a human trial, or is it a mouse study wearing a human headline?

What has real (if early) human data

NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide). This is arguably the best-studied compound in the category right now. Small human trials, generally 8–12 weeks and a few hundred participants, show NMN reliably raises NAD+ levels and is well tolerated, with some studies showing modest improvements in insulin sensitivity and aerobic capacity in older adults. What it hasn't shown: that raising NAD+ actually extends human lifespan. That link is still a hypothesis, not a demonstrated result.

Resveratrol. This one's a cautionary tale about hype outrunning data. It was identified as a sirtuin activator by Harvard researchers and became the face of "longevity gene" science — but human trials have been underwhelming, and its oral bioavailability is under 1%, meaning very little of what you swallow actually gets absorbed. It's often paired with NMN on the theory that sirtuins need NAD+ as fuel, which is mechanistically reasonable, but that combination itself hasn't been validated in a dedicated human longevity trial.

Omega-3 (DHA). Less exciting, much more solid. Long-standing evidence for cardiovascular and cognitive benefits, which matter for healthspan even without a dramatic "anti-aging" mechanism attached.

What's promising but not there yet

Fisetin and spermidine are the ones to watch. Fisetin has good senolytic (senescent-cell-clearing) data in animals and has only recently moved into small human safety/tolerability trials — encouraging, but far from proof of effect. Spermidine has an interesting autophagy-activation mechanism but is in the same boat: mechanistically plausible, human evidence still thin. Quercetin, despite popularity, has poor bioavailability and no longevity-specific human trials to date.

What to leave to a doctor, not a supplement stack

Metformin and rapamycin show up constantly in longevity discussions, but they're prescription medications with real risk profiles, not supplements — sourcing them outside a physician's supervision is a genuinely bad idea, not just an "evidence gap" issue.

The honest bottom line

No supplement has been shown in a randomized controlled trial to extend human lifespan. Not one. What the current evidence supports is narrower and less exciting: some compounds (NMN, omega-3) plausibly support the systems that tend to fail first — metabolic, cardiovascular, muscular — in people who already have a decent baseline (sleep, training, protein intake, blood work). Supplements are additive to that foundation, not a substitute for it.

If you're going to experiment, the more defensible approach is: pick compounds with actual human RCT data, track a real biomarker (bloodwork, VO2 max, a biological-age test) before and after, and loop in a physician — especially for anything that interacts with existing medications or conditions.

This is a summary of published research, not medical advice — talk to a clinician before starting any supplement, especially if you're on medication or managing a health condition.

FAQ

Do any longevity supplements have proven human lifespan benefits?

No. No supplement has been shown in a randomized controlled trial to extend human lifespan. Compounds like NMN and omega-3 have supportive human data for narrower outcomes such as NAD+ levels, insulin sensitivity, and cardiovascular markers.

Is resveratrol worth taking for longevity?

The evidence is weaker than its reputation suggests. Human trials have been underwhelming and its oral bioavailability is under 1%, so very little of an oral dose is actually absorbed.

Can I take rapamycin or metformin for anti-aging?

Not without a doctor. Both are prescription medications with real risk profiles — rapamycin is an immunosuppressant — and self-sourcing them outside medical supervision is not advisable.

What's the most defensible way to try a longevity supplement?

Pick compounds with actual human trial data, track a real biomarker before and after (bloodwork, VO2 max, or a biological-age test), and involve a physician, especially if you take other medications or manage a health condition.
